How beatable are the players?

average player whole population, as one mixed strategy where theory says learning settles

Exploitability is how much a perfect opponent could win from you, computed exactly over all 64 possible quitting times. With luck-driven learning it levels off above 0. Independent learners also drift apart (some quit early, some late) while the group as a whole stays near the ideal. With a fixed pair of 2, one player usually ends up always giving in.

Inside player 1's brain

A waiting contest where both sides pay

Picture two kids who both want the last swing. Neither pushes; they just wait. Every minute costs both of them, and the moment one walks away the other gets the swing. Game theorists call this a war of attrition. Male fruit flies play it when they fight over food.

Rule 1

Both pay while it lasts

Every second of fighting costs each player 1, no matter who ends up winning.

Rule 2

First to quit loses

The moment one player gives up, the other gets the prize (worth 1 in Game A).

Rule 3

The winner still paid

A long fight can cost more than the prize is worth, so holding on forever is a bad plan too.

Can you beat the perfect strategy?

Why quitting at random is unbeatable

If you always quit at the same moment, a clever opponent simply waits one tick longer and wins every time. So the best plan is to be unpredictable, in a very particular way: at every moment keep the same small chance of giving up, however long the fight has lasted.

That makes every quitting time equally good for your opponent, so they have nothing to exploit. Mathematically the quitting time follows an exponential law, P(still fighting at t) = e−t, mean 1 second. The chart on the left shows it: against the perfect quitter the payoff line is flat, meaning no choice beats any other.

Ventimiglia et al. (2026) timed thousands of real fruit-fly fights and found this same exponential pattern in when the loser gives up.

Game B: when each player wants it differently

Each player privately knows how much it values the prize, v. The unbeatable rule is to wait exactly v²/2 seconds: the more you want it, the longer you hold on, and the faster your patience grows. A fly that barely cares (v = 0.5) should leave after 0.125 s; one that really wants it (v = 3) should hold on for 4.5 s.

What if losses teach more than wins?

Real flies take losing harder than winning. If learners copy that, they no longer settle on the perfect strategy. The math (worked out before any experiment) predicts where they settle instead: more players quit at once, and a few hold on much longer.

The fly's give-up relay

Scientists found that a fly gives up when a small relay of brain cells switches off its aggression cells. The whole male fruit-fly nervous system has been mapped cell by cell (the MaleCNS connectome), so we cut that relay out of the map with every real connection intact.

The aggression cells that keep a fly fighting. The players' quitting strategy is read out here.

That exact circuit has 81 cells, so we added the 177 cells most strongly connected to it (one step out), giving 258 cells. Kenyon cells, the mushroom body's main input, are left out because no single one is strongly connected.

The two fair copies

Same cells, same number of connections, same size of brain. The shuffled copy even keeps how many connections each cell has, and only changes who connects to whom. The real wiring is far more reciprocal (cells that talk back to each other) and clustered than either copy.

What the full study found Fly wiring made no difference to how well the players learned.

Across both games and both kinds of practice, players with the real fly circuit ended up exactly as close to the perfect strategy as players with the shuffled or random copies. The fly-like learning rule (losses count more) mattered a lot; the fly wiring did not.

Every kind of brain moved away from the perfect strategy by about the amount the math predicted, plus the usual noise floor. It also produced a small loser effect: after losing, players quit a little earlier. Wiring changed neither.

How the study worked

  • Agents: learning players, each with its own brain, choosing when to quit. Two ways to practise: exact self-play (noise-free) and a population of 8 players who learn only from their own fights (multi-agent reinforcement learning, REINFORCE with a baseline).
  • Fair comparison: every network brain has the same number of adjustable settings (5,584 to 5,610), and each is tested against a shuffled copy that keeps every cell's number of connections, and a random copy.
  • Score: exploitability, how much a perfect opponent could win from you, computed exactly. 10 to 20 repeated runs per setting, reported with 95% ranges and corrected for many comparisons.
  • Honest limits: practising by playing never gets exploitability all the way to 0 (luck); only one way of cutting out the circuit was tested; the go/stop signs are predictions from the brain images.
