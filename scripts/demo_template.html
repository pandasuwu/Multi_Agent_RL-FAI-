A game player wired from a fly’s brain.

Male fruit flies fight over food, and one known circuit decides when a fly gives up. I took that circuit out of the fly connectome and made it the brain of agents playing the same game, to see if its wiring helps them play well.

The game

Two flies, one bit of food. Both pay for every second they keep fighting, and the one that quits first gets nothing. This is the war of attrition from game theory.

There’s no single best quitting time. The best play is to quit at random, with a mix of times that leaves no fly able to do better by changing. These two are drawn from the live population further down, so they’re still learning.

The circuit

A 2026 study traced what makes a fly give up: PPL1 dopamine neurons, then the MBON18/19 output neurons of the mushroom body, then the Tk neurons (AVLP727m) that keep it fighting. I cut this out of the MaleCNS connectome along with its closest partners.

The situation goes in at PPL1. The signal spreads for six steps, only along connections the fly actually has, and the decision is read from the five Tk neurons.

To check whether the wiring itself matters, the same neurons also get fake wiring. Shuffled keeps how many connections each neuron has but moves where they go. Random puts the same number of connections anywhere.

Real against fake, learning now

Three groups of eight players learn in your browser, one group per wiring. Each group starts from the same random weights and learns only from the fights it plays. The score is exploitability: how much a player could still win by changing its strategy. Zero is perfect play.

Result: the wiring didn’t matter

In both games, whether agents learned from exact payoffs or only from their own fights, the real circuit finished where the shuffled and random wiring did. Each bar is the real circuit’s final exploitability minus a fake wiring’s, with a 95% interval, all at one shared learning rate. Left of zero would mean the real wiring helped.

The only effect of the real wiring I could find is speed. In exact Game A it gets to the target 15 to 20 episodes sooner, out of about 230, and that goes away once connection signs follow Dale’s law.

Other choices mattered more. In Game B, reading the decision from all 258 neurons instead of the five Tk neurons closed most of the gap to an ordinary neural network. With only two players, one usually learns to give in at once and the pair barely fights; from eight players on, the group settles on the random best strategy.

Agents that count losses more than wins, the way flies seem to, quit earlier. They end further from the best strategy but keep more than twice as much of the prize, because the best strategy is wasteful: in Game A the fighting burns the whole prize, and in Game B the waiting costs exactly what sorting the food to the hungrier fly gains.
